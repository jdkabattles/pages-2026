Freelancer Budget and Pricing Template: Track Work and Price a Job

A freelancer's budget is not a guess about next month. It is a record of what came in, what went out, and what each job actually cost in time and expenses. This guide describes a simple spreadsheet structure you can build yourself. It is general information only.

Not personal financial advice. This page does not give tax advice, investment advice, or advice for your specific situation, and it makes no earnings claim. For tax, legal, or benefits questions, consult a qualified professional in your country.

What to track, every month

  • Income: client, job, date invoiced, date paid, amount, and payment method.
  • Costs: software, equipment, transport, phone and internet share used for work, and any subcontractor or licence costs.
  • Time: hours per job, including revisions and messages, not just the headline task.
  • Unpaid work: quotes, pitches, and admin time, so you can see how much of your week is billable.

Spreadsheet structure

Use four sheets: Jobs (one row per job, with client, scope, price, status, and paid date), Income (one row per payment received), Costs (one row per cost, with category and date), and Summary (totals by month, pulled from the other sheets, not typed by hand). Keep formulas in the Summary sheet so a single corrected row updates the totals.

How to price a job

  1. Scope it in writing: what is included, how many revisions, and what counts as extra work.
  2. Estimate the time honestly: include research, revisions, delivery, and messages.
  3. Add direct costs: licences, stock, travel, or anything you buy for that job.
  4. Add a buffer for unpaid time: quoting, admin, and gaps between jobs are real costs of freelancing.
  5. Quote a price, not just a rate: a project price tied to scope is easier for a client to approve than an open-ended hourly guess — but only if the scope is clear.

Common mistakes

  • Pricing from the headline task alone and forgetting revisions and messages.
  • Mixing personal and business money in one account or one sheet.
  • Waiting until year-end to record income and costs.
  • Accepting extra work without updating the price or the deadline in writing.
